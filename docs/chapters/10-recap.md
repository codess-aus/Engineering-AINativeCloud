## The thread through every chapter

Nine chapters, one constant: **a human owns every merge, no matter how many agents were involved in
producing it.** Everything else — the Fleet and Squad patterns, the review pipeline, the control plane, the
backlog triage — exists to make that ownership sustainable at higher volume, not to dilute it.

- **Human in control.** Rule Zero doesn't get renegotiated as agents get more capable. The accountable
  person for a merged change is always named, always human ([Chapter 2](02-rule-zero.md), [Chapter 3](03-accountability.md)).
- **Agents do the work.** Scoped, well-specified, low-ambiguity work is exactly what agents absorb well —
  and absorbing it well frees judgement-heavy work for the humans who are best placed to do it
  ([Chapter 4](04-replaceable.md)).
- **Outcomes that compound.** Fleet and Squad operating models, used deliberately rather than as a single
  undifferentiated "AI assistant," let throughput scale without losing the review rigour that quality depends
  on ([Chapter 5](05-fleet-squad.md), [Chapter 7](07-demo.md)).
- **Trust that scales.** The backlog has no bottom, and the business cliff rewards teams that can safely run
  more concurrent work — which only holds if governance capacity grows alongside generation capacity
  ([Chapter 6](06-backlog.md), [Chapter 8](08-business-cliff.md)).
- **Culture that endures.** A model-agnostic control plane means the governance discipline survives every
  future model change, vendor shift, or new agent capability — because it was never tied to a specific tool
  in the first place ([Chapter 9](09-control-plane.md)).

## The multiplier and the guardrail

Judgement is the multiplier: an expert directing ten agents, accountable for all ten outcomes, produces more
than ten engineers working independently without that coordinating judgement — but only if governance is
the guardrail that keeps the multiplication safe. Remove the guardrail and the multiplier becomes a
liability generator instead. Both halves of that sentence matter equally; the talk (and this site) spends
as much time on the guardrail as the multiplier because that's the half that's easiest to skip under
delivery pressure.

## Where to go from here

If you're starting this shift on your own team, the practical order that this site's chapters suggest is:
write your Rule Zero statement (Chapter 2), harden your review gate (Chapter 3), pick one low-risk backlog
tier to trial (Chapter 6), run it end to end with an agent reviewer in the loop (Chapters 5 and 7), and only
then start scaling to more concurrent projects (Chapter 8) with a control plane view across all of them
(Chapter 9).

The [Resources](../resources.md) page collects every link used across this site, plus further reading on
agentic engineering practice.
